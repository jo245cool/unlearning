About the project

About

Unlearning began with a simple question: what have you come to believe differently? It has since become more interesting in the harder cases.

The idea

Most conversations about changed minds settle for a clean story — someone believed one thing, life happened, now they believe another. Unlearning starts, but doesn't stop there. It's just as interested in beliefs that weaken slowly instead of breaking, values that quietly compete with each other, the gap between what someone believes and how they actually live, ideas that survive intact despite everything that should have unsettled them, and the kinds of change a person might not even recognize in themselves.

The goal isn't to catch anyone being inconsistent, and it isn't to turn every conversation into a redemption arc from wrong to right. It's to sit with how tangled the relationship between belief, experience, and identity actually is — and to let that tangle stay a little tangled on the page.

How the conversations happen

There's no fixed opening question, and interviews don't necessarily start with someone naming a belief they used to hold. Each conversation begins wherever genuine curiosity is — a question I don't already know the answer to — and develops from there based on what the person actually says, not a prepared list of follow-ups.

Nothing about what a person's story is supposed to demonstrate is decided beforehand. Whatever patterns, tensions, contradictions, or shapes of change turn up are identified afterward, in the Reflection — not steered toward during the conversation itself.

Conversation and Reflection

Every entry in the archive is split into two parts. The Conversation is the interviewee's own account, edited for readability but not for polish — it aims to preserve how someone actually spoke, not to make them sound more articulate or more philosophically tidy than they were. The Reflection that follows is something else entirely: it's the interviewer's reading of the conversation, not the interviewee's. It's kept separate on purpose, so a reader can form their own sense of things first, and only afterward see one possible interpretation — offered as one reading among others, not a verdict.

Editorial note

Conversations published here are edited for length and clarity. Repetition, false starts, and material unrelated to the conversation's throughline are typically removed. Interviews conducted in a language other than English may be translated. In every case, editing is meant to preserve meaning, not shift it — nothing is added, and nothing is reframed to say something the speaker didn't.

The distinction between the two halves of each entry matters: the Conversation is the interviewee's account of their own experience. The Reflection is the interviewer's interpretation of that account, offered afterward and clearly marked as separate, not as an extension of what the interviewee themselves said.

A growing archive

Unlearning is not a finished argument. It's built to keep adding conversations, and to let new ones complicate or reshape whatever the earlier ones seemed to suggest. The themes noted throughout the archive — contradiction, identity, family, morality, certainty, circumstance, knowledge — are offered loosely, as threads a reader might follow, not as a taxonomy the project is trying to prove.
