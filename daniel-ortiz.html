UL–001

Daniel Ortiz

Daniel grew up in a household where political conviction was close to a family trait — inherited, defended, rarely revisited. Ten years working as a paramedic put him in rooms with people his upbringing had taught him firm opinions about. This conversation is about what happened to those opinions, and what didn't.

The Conversation

What's something you believe differently now than you did ten years ago?

Almost everything about who deserves patience. I grew up in a house where you could tell, before someone finished a sentence, whether they were "one of us" or not. And once you knew that, you already knew what to think of them. I didn't question it because nobody around me questioned it — it wasn't really a belief, it was more like furniture. It was just there.

When did that start to change?

Not on one call. That's the honest answer — people want a single night that changed me, and there wasn't one. But there was a stretch, maybe eighteen months in, where I noticed I'd stopped being able to guess who was "one of us" before I got to a patient's door. It didn't matter anymore. I'd walk in and just see a person who was scared or in pain, and everything I thought I knew about them in advance turned out to be useless information. It didn't help me do my job even a little.

Did that change how you thought about the people you grew up around?

That's the part I'm still not settled on. I don't think they're bad people. I think they've just never had a version of their life where the categories stopped being useful to them. I still love my dad. I still think he's wrong about a lot. Those two things don't cancel out, they just sit next to each other now, and I've stopped waiting for one of them to win.

Is there anything from back then you still believe, even though you can't fully defend it?

That people mostly get what they work for. I know the job has shown me exactly how untrue that is, over and over. I still catch myself thinking it about strangers sometimes, in the first half-second before I know anything about them. I don't like that about myself. I haven't gotten rid of it, I've just gotten faster at noticing it and putting it down.

Reflection

What stands out in this conversation isn't the change Daniel names outright — the erosion of an inherited, categorical way of sizing people up. It's the belief he flags as unresolved: that people mostly get what they work for. He can identify it, distrust it, and watch it activate in himself anyway, in the half-second before conscious thought catches up.

That's a useful case for Unlearning, because it resists the tidy version of this project's own premise. Daniel's story isn't "I used to believe X, now I believe Y." It's closer to: one belief was replaced through sustained, repeated contact with evidence against it, while a structurally similar belief — about desert and effort — has survived the same decade of contact largely intact, just quieter and more monitored.

It raises a question worth sitting with rather than resolving here: why does direct experience dislodge some beliefs and only discipline others? One possibility is that the paramedic work gave him a substitute for the first belief — a new, working framework (everyone is just a person in a bad moment) that could replace the old one wholesale. The second belief may have no ready replacement waiting for it, so it persists not because it survived scrutiny, but because nothing else has yet earned the space it occupies.

I don't think that's a flaw in Daniel's account. If anything it's the most honest part of it — the admission that noticing a belief is wrong and being rid of it are two different jobs, and the second one is slower.
