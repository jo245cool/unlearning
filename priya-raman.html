UL–003

Priya Raman

Priya was raised to believe that family obligation was not a negotiable value but a settled fact, like gravity. At thirty-four she left the family business her grandfather had started. This conversation is about the belief she thought was settled, and what she's found underneath it since.

FamilyCertainty
Conversation Reflection
The Conversation

How certain were you, growing up, that you'd spend your life in the business?

Completely certain. Not hopeful, not expected to — certain, the way you're certain the sun comes up. It wasn't presented as a choice I'd make later. It was presented as a fact about who I already was. I don't think I ever once, as a kid, imagined a version of my life without it in it.

What was it like when that certainty started to move?

Slow, and honestly kind of humiliating. I didn't have a moment where I realized I wanted out. I had years of small resentments I wasn't allowed to name, because naming them would have meant admitting the "fact about who I was" was actually just a story I'd been told very consistently. It's hard to describe how disorienting it is to realize a certainty was never really yours to begin with.

You said before we started recording that you still don't know if leaving was the right call. Can you say more about that?

I left, and I'm relieved, and I also think I broke something that can't really be fixed — not the business, my father. He's proud of me in the sentences he says out loud and I don't think he's proud of me underneath them. Both of those are true at the same time. I used to want one feeling to be the "real" one so I could just have a clean answer. I've stopped expecting that.

If you had a daughter, would you raise her the way you were raised?

No. But I want to be careful here, because it's easy to say that and sound like I think my parents did something monstrous. I don't think that. I think they were as certain about my obligation as I once was, and they were passing on something they'd also been handed as settled fact. I'd want to raise a kid who knows obligation is a real thing worth taking seriously — just not one that arrives pre-decided.

Reflection

Priya's account complicates a distinction this project leans on more than it probably should: the line between a belief changing and a belief being revealed as something that was never quite a belief at all. She describes her sense of obligation not as a conviction she came to doubt, but as an inherited certainty she eventually recognized had never been hers to examine in the first place — closer to an unexamined premise than a considered position.

That reframes what "unlearning" means in her case. It isn't only the replacement of one belief with another. It's also the slower, separate work of noticing that something presented as settled fact was actually a claim — one she'd never been offered the chance to agree or disagree with — and only then becoming able to hold it up for inspection at all.

The unresolved tension she names at the end is worth sitting with rather than tidying up: she rejects the mechanism by which the obligation was handed to her, without rejecting the value of obligation itself. She's not arguing her parents were wrong to feel bound by duty. She's arguing that duty arriving pre-decided, rather than chosen, is the part that failed her — which leaves open a harder question this conversation doesn't answer: how anyone tells the difference, from the inside, between a value they hold and a value they were simply never permitted to question.
