UL–002

Tomás Ferreira

For most of his twenties and thirties, Tomás measured a good life almost entirely by output. Then his mother's illness pulled him out of work for a year. This conversation, conducted partly in Portuguese and translated here, is about what he found on the other side of that year — and what he's still not sure he believes.

The Conversation

What did ambition mean to you before that year?

Honestly, it meant almost everything. I thought a life justified itself by what it produced. If you weren't building something — a career, a body of work, a name — you were wasting the time you'd been given. I didn't think of that as a philosophy, exactly. It just felt like the obvious way to keep score.

What changed when your mother got sick?

I stopped working, almost overnight, to take care of her. And for the first few months I felt like I was disappearing. Nothing I did that year would show up on a résumé. I made soup. I sat with her through nights she was scared. I learned how to argue gently with a doctor. None of it was building anything I could point to later.

And now, looking back on it?

Now I think that year might have been the most useful one of my life, and I can't fully explain why, because it produced nothing by my old definition of the word. I keep reaching for the old scorecard to explain why it mattered, and it doesn't fit. So either the scorecard was wrong, or I still haven't found the right words for what actually happened.

Do you still feel the pull of the old way of measuring things?

Constantly. I went back to work after, and within about two months I was right back to feeling anxious on weekends I hadn't produced anything. I don't think a year fixes twenty. But now there's a second voice that shows up when the anxious one starts talking, and it just asks: produced anything for whom, exactly? I don't always have an answer, but the question didn't used to be there at all.

Reflection

Tomás is careful, more than once, not to overclaim the change. He doesn't say the caregiving year converted him to a new value system. He says it introduced a second voice that argues with the old one — and that the old one still mostly wins on an ordinary weekend. That distinction is worth taking seriously rather than smoothing over into a cleaner story of transformation.

What makes this a useful entry for the archive is the mismatch he names directly: the year he now considers most useful is also the year that produced nothing by the measure he'd used his whole adult life to judge usefulness. Rather than resolving that mismatch by simply adopting a new definition of a good life, he's left holding two incompatible scorekeeping systems at once, one of which he no longer fully trusts but hasn't discarded.

It's tempting to read this as a belief in transition — the old value fading, the new one arriving — and it might be. But it's just as consistent with a belief that survives indefinitely alongside a competing one, each dominant in different circumstances: the old scorecard governing ordinary weeks, something closer to the caregiving year's logic surfacing only under duress. If that's right, the "second voice" isn't necessarily the seed of future change. It may simply be what conflict between two live values sounds like from the inside, with no guarantee either one is going to win.
